## Rules

### workflow

- **plan-first.** Work in this order: footprint and circulation, then masses and heights, then roof, then palette, then depth and detail, then lighting and surroundings. *Starting from the facade or from random blocks gives boxes that cannot be fixed with decoration.*
- **theme-first.** Pick one style (see styles) and its palette before placing the first block; every later choice follows from it. Local materials win over taste: a stone castle needs a quarry, a desert town is sandstone. *Mixed styles and out-of-place materials read as noise.*
- **fill-then-carve.** Block out each volume with fill_box (or hollow boxes), then carve openings and add detail. Prefer a few big fills to many single blocks. *Single-block placement is slow, error-prone and runs into per-call limits.*
- **review-loop.** After each stage look at the result (screenshot) and run the build review on the area; fix what it flags before adding more. *Problems compound when detail is added on top of a bad base.*

### palette

- **palette-size.** Use 3 to 5 building blocks: a base (about half of the surface), a secondary (about a third) and an accent (about a fifth). More than 5 materials looks messy; one material looks flat. *Too many materials: chaos. One material: a featureless box.*
- **palette-contrast.** Give frame and infill different tones or textures: a darker or lighter frame (logs, dark oak) on lighter walls, or stone below and wood above. Similar blocks look fine up close but vanish at a distance. *Walls and frame of the same block have no readable structure.*
- **palette-gradient.** Use dark below and light above (plinth darker than walls, walls darker than trim). Grade 3 or 4 blocks of one hue from light to dark instead of a single block. *Light-on-top draws the eye up and gives weight to the base.*
- **palette-texture-mix.** Mix variants of one family in small, justified amounts: stone bricks with a few cracked or mossy ones near the ground and the corners. Never 50/50 random noise; keep the odd variant to roughly one block in ten, grouped where weathering would happen. *Evenly scattered variants read as static, not age.*
- **palette-rare-accent.** Rare or loud blocks (gold, emerald, diamond, quartz, coloured glass, glowstone) are accents at doors, corners, ridges and focal windows, never the bulk. *Whole walls of shiny blocks look cheap.*
- **palette-whole-building.** Roof, ceiling, floor and furniture follow the same palette as the walls. A ceiling in a clashing wood or a glass floor breaks the interior.

### silhouette

- **silhouette-not-box.** Break the cube: use an L, T or U footprint, two or three volumes of different heights, a porch, a tower or a wing. One dominant volume, smaller ones subordinate. *A plain rectangle with a roof is the signature of a first attempt.*
- **silhouette-asymmetry.** Keep an ordered frame but add controlled asymmetry: an offset chimney, a lean-to, a door off the centre line. Use odd widths when something must sit exactly in the middle (single door, ridge). *Perfect symmetry everywhere is sterile; random asymmetry is sloppy.*
- **silhouette-heights.** Vary floor and ceiling heights between rooms and wings; do not put every wall at the same height.

### depth

- **depth-relief.** No facade is a single plane. Every 3 to 5 blocks change the depth by one block: recess a window bay, push out a pillar or a corner, add a bay window or a balcony. *Flat walls catch no shadow and look painted on.*
- **depth-windows.** Set windows one block back from the wall face (or push them out as a bay), frame them with trapdoors, stairs or slabs, and give them a sill and a lintel. *Glass flush with the wall disappears.*
- **depth-trim.** Add a cornice (upside-down stairs or slabs) under the roof, a plinth where the wall meets the ground, and vertical pillars at the corners and every 3 to 5 blocks.
- **depth-3d-texture.** Detail with small shaped blocks: trapdoors as shutters, fences as railings and posts, walls as columns, stairs and slabs as ledges, buttons or ladders as texture. Never leave a large wall of one flat block.

### walls

- **walls-thickness.** Exterior walls are 2 to 3 blocks thick for stone and castle styles (window reveals need it); light timber walls are one block over a frame. Interior partitions are one block. *A one-block stone wall cannot be recessed.*
- **walls-two-tone.** Decorate plain walls with one of: two tones (dark low, light high), a band of another material, a wavy boundary between two materials (one block step), or 3D texture. Pick one to four wall elements and use them everywhere in the room or facade.
- **walls-taper.** Large castles and churches get thicker at the bottom: step the base out by one block or add buttresses (stairs-capped piers) at regular spacing. Small houses keep a constant thickness.
- **walls-battlements.** For crenellations alternate a full block and a gap along the top edge; add corner towers that stand out from the wall.

### roof

- **roof-slope.** Pitched roofs are the default: 1:1 (stairs, 45 degrees) is the standard, 1:2 (alternating slabs) is flatter, 2:1 (two blocks per step) is steep. Use a flat roof only for the modern style, and give it a parapet or an edge of a different block. *A flat roof with no edge looks unfinished and lets mobs spawn.*
- **roof-overhang.** Let the roof overhang the walls by one block (two for big roofs); it throws a shadow line and protects the facade.
- **roof-height.** A roof is one to two storeys tall. Past about 5 to 7 blocks per section, split it into several roofs, put a storey in the attic, or turn the volume into a tower. A single plain gable wider than about 12 to 15 blocks looks like a barn: use several gables (T, L, cross-gable) or a hip roof.
- **roof-parity.** Odd span: the ridge is a single block row centred over the middle (and over a single door). Even span: two stairs back to back, or two top slabs, form the ridge. *Mismatched parity leaves a one-block gap or a lopsided ridge.*
- **roof-gables.** Close the gable ends with wall material (a triangle), put one window in the middle of it, and break big roofs with dormers or a chimney.
- **roof-joins.** When joining roofs build the tallest one first as if alone, then the next, down to the lowest; finally remove the blocks that ended up inside another roof.
- **roof-contrast.** Make the roof a contrasting material from the walls (dark stairs on light walls), still within the palette.

### windows

- **windows-size.** Windows start one to two blocks above the floor. Vary their size and spacing; avoid a perfect grid. A tall 1x2 window gets trapdoor shutters; big openings (3 wide) suit halls.
- **windows-glass.** Use glass panes for thin, framed windows and full glass blocks for modern walls; tint glass to match the mood (blue near water, white for a cloudy look). Leave an opening rather than a glass wall in styles that would not have glass.
- **windows-doors.** Few doors, framed with a lintel, a step and a lamp. A door is two blocks tall and sits flush with the wall or in a recess.

### interior

- **interior-height.** Ceilings are 3 to 4 blocks in a house (a slab ceiling gives about 2.5), 4 to 5 in a small room, 5 to 6 in a medieval hall, 10 to 12 in a castle great hall. Vary heights from room to room. *A 2-block ceiling cannot be jumped under and feels cramped.*
- **interior-halls.** Corridors are 2 blocks wide in a small house and 3 to 4 in a large one; add a carpet and a window.
- **interior-floors.** Floors are never bare dirt or grass. Alternate two blocks (planks and logs, polished andesite and diorite, a chequer of two concretes), vary by room (tile in kitchen and bathroom, wood in living rooms, cobblestone in cellars), and put a pattern in the middle of a room with a plain border.
- **interior-furniture.** Furnish with shaped blocks: a table is a fence with a pressure plate on top, a chair is a stair, a sofa 2 to 4 stairs, shelves are bookshelves, a cabinet is a barrel with trapdoors. Put a focal point in each room (hearth, table, bookshelf wall).
- **interior-hearth.** A fireplace is a stone or brick frame with a 1x2 opening, a campfire or netherrack as fire, and a chimney that rises through the roof. Keep wood out of the 4 blocks above a fire. *Wood near fire burns.*

### light

- **light-plan.** Light every room and the outside path at roughly one source every 6 to 8 blocks; hide sources in the structure (under carpets, in slabs, behind panes, in lamps) rather than torches stuck on walls. *Dark corners spawn mobs and look dull.*
- **light-style.** Match the light to the style: torches and campfires for rustic and medieval, glowstone, sea lanterns or redstone lamps for modern and underwater, shroomlight for the Nether, froglights for fantasy.

### terrain

- **terrain-integrate.** Fit the building to the ground: dig into slopes, step the foundation, never leave a flat square of grass at the foot. Add a path, a garden, a fence, hedges (leaves on a log), a well or a tree group of two or three trees. *A building dropped on a lawn looks pasted.*
- **terrain-paths.** Paths are 2 to 3 blocks wide, mix two or three path blocks (gravel, cobblestone, coarse dirt, andesite), wiggle slightly, and lead to the door. Village streets radiate from a centre (well, square) and buildings adapt to them.
- **terrain-water.** Water features need a rim: a fountain is a frame at least 5x5 with a column in the middle and a margin of 4 blocks around it; a cascade needs a barrier above its top and a trench at its foot; a pool is dug 1 deep everywhere first and deeper in zones.

### scale

- **scale-reference.** Reference sizes (width x height x depth): small starter house 7x5x7, medium house 11x6x9, large manor 17x8x15, barn 9x6x11, tower 5x12x5, bridge 5 wide with an arch every 7 blocks. Too small a building cannot hold detail.
- **scale-hierarchy.** In a settlement make landmarks bigger and taller, keep houses modest, leave open space between buildings, and keep industry away from homes.

### logic

- **logic-materials.** Choose blocks for what they would be in the world: stone or cobblestone for foundations, wood for frames and floors, brick or stone for chimneys and fireplaces, glass for windows, never glass or wool for a floor or a barn wall, never leaves or sand as walls. *Illogical materials destroy believability faster than any other mistake.*
- **logic-fire.** Wood, wool and carpet burn. Keep them away from fire, lava and campfires; use stone, brick or concrete around hearths.
- **logic-loose.** Dirt, grass, sand, gravel and snow are ground, not building blocks: they fall, can be carried off by endermen and read as unfinished. Use them for paths, gardens and terrain.
- **logic-aging.** Age follows story: new buildings use clean blocks, old ones add cracked and mossy variants, broken windows and vines, and overgrown ruins use mossy stone and holes. Add it deliberately and only where water and shade would put it.

## Common mistakes

| mistake | fix |
|---|---|
| Plain box: one block, smooth walls, flat roof. | Break the footprint, add a pitched roof with an overhang, change materials by role and add relief. |
| More than five materials. | Reduce to a base, a secondary and one or two accents; use shades of the same family. |
| Perfect symmetry on every face. | Keep the plan ordered and add one or two deliberate off-centre elements. |
| Wall one block thick with glass flush to it. | Thicken to two or three where possible, recess glass one block and frame it. |
| Flat roof with no edge, or a roof that is too big for the walls. | Add a parapet or a pitched roof of 1 to 2 storeys, split big roofs into sections. |
| Random scatter of cracked and mossy blocks. | Group variants near the ground and corners, one block in ten. |
| Glass, wool, dirt or sand where they are illogical. | Use planks or stone for floors and walls; keep loose blocks for the ground. |
| Torches on every wall. | Hide light in the structure or use proper lamps; light the way to the door. |
| Building pasted on a flat lawn. | Dig into the slope, add a path, a plinth, hedges, trees and a garden. |
| Too small to carry detail. | Start from the reference sizes and scale up. |
| Starting with the facade or with details. | Footprint, masses, roof, palette, then relief and detail. |

## Roof types

| roof | use | recipe |
|---|---|---|
| `gable` | The default pitched roof, two slopes meeting at a ridge; good up to about 12 blocks of span. | Stairs on both long walls facing the ridge, shifted one block in and one up per layer; ridge block or two back-to-back stairs; fill the triangular end walls. Tool: build_plan kind=gable_roof. |
| `hip` | Slopes on all four sides; little attic volume; a pyramid on a square plan. | Concentric rings of stairs facing inward, shrinking one block per layer, corner stairs shaped automatically; cap with a block or slab. Tool: build_plan kind=hip_roof. |
| `shed` | One slope, for lean-tos, porches and sheds. | A single run of stairs all facing up the slope; tuck its high side against a wall. Tool: build_plan kind=shed_roof. |
| `flat` | Modern style only. | A slab or block layer with a parapet one block high (or an edge of stairs or slabs of another material); light or cover it so mobs do not spawn. |
| `saltbox` | A gable with one slope much longer than the other, often over a rear lean-to. | Two runs of different length; the long slope may be shallower. |
| `gambrel` | Barns and large volumes: steep lower slope, shallow upper slope. | A steep run (2:1) then a gentle run (1:1 or 1:2); only above about 16x20. |
| `mansard` | Like a gambrel but on four sides, usually with dormers. | Steep on all four sides to a shallow or flat top ringed by a parapet; needs a big plan. |
| `pyramid` | Towers and pavilions. | Hip roof on a square plan up to a single block. |
| `cone` | Round towers. | Disc layers shrinking one block of radius every one or two levels, edges of stairs or slabs. Tool: build_plan kind=cone. |
| `dome` | Domes, observatories, mosques. | Upper half of a sphere layer by layer. Tool: build_plan kind=dome. |
| `thatch` | Rustic cottages. | One uninterrupted steep slope in a single material (hay or yellow carpet); no valleys, eyebrow dormers only. |

## Furniture

Built from shaped blocks the engine has.

| piece | recipe |
|---|---|
| table | A fence post with a pressure plate on top (keep it off the wall so the fence does not connect), or two stairs back to back. |
| long table | A row of fence posts every 2 blocks with slabs on top, or slabs on a stair base at both ends. |
| chair | One stair facing the table (or a slab with trapdoor arms). |
| sofa | 2 to 4 stairs side by side facing the room, a stair turned inward at each end as arms. |
| bookshelf wall | Stacked bookshelves 2 to 3 high, a slab or trapdoor row as the top lip. |
| cabinet | A barrel (or bookshelf) with a trapdoor in front and one on top. |
| lamp | A fence with a torch on top, or a light block hidden under a carpet in the floor. |
| counter | A run of slabs or stairs of stone in front of a crafting table, furnace or smoker. |
| hearth | A stone or brick frame around a 1x2x1 opening with a campfire or netherrack, chimney to the roof. |
| rug | A 3x5 area of carpets in two colours with a border. |
| shutter | A trapdoor on each side of a window, opened flat against the wall. |
| flower pot | A trapdoor box around a block of dirt with a plant on top. |

Floor patterns:

- log and planks stripes (alternate rows)
- polished andesite and polished diorite chequer (kitchen, bathroom)
- two concretes in a chequer or concentric bands
- stone bricks mixed with a few cracked, mossy and cobblestone (cellar, ruin)
- smooth stone border around a central motif

## Limits of the block set

- Stairs, slabs, doors, trapdoors, fences, walls, panes, carpets, torches and ladders exist as shaped blocks (blockset shapes). Only about 38 materials have stairs and 40 have slabs: check the palette, not the vanilla list (no stairs for deepslate, tuff, mud brick, concrete, wool, cherry, bamboo, mangrove).
- There is no double slab: use the full block. No lantern, chain, candle, bed, sign, item frame, painting, chest, rail or crops; use glowstone, sea lanterns, redstone lamps, torches, barrels and bookshelves.
- Stair corners, fence, wall and pane connections are derived from the neighbours: never place corner states by hand.
- Orientable blocks (furnace, barrel, ...) always face +z (south).
- Stairs: facing is the direction you climb toward (the tall back is on that side). half=top flips them upside down (cornices, eaves). A bare name is the default state (facing north, bottom).
- Doors are two blocks (lower and upper half); use the bare name for the default state or name each half.
